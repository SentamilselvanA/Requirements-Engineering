1. Introduction & Learning Objectives

What is Requirements Engineering? Requirements Engineering (RE) is the systematic process of discovering, documenting, analyzing, validating, and managing the needs and constraints that a system must satisfy. It bridges the gap between stakeholder expectations and the technical design of a system.

Why RE Matters in Embedded Systems

Embedded systems operate in physical environments with strict constraints on timing, power, memory, safety, and hardware interfaces. A missing or ambiguous requirement can cause a product recall, safety hazard, or costly redesign. Studies show that fixing a defect found during requirements costs 10–100× less than fixing it after deployment.

🎯 Objectives

  • Define and document stakeholder needs
  • Translate needs into verifiable specifications
  • Manage changes systematically
  • Provide a baseline for design and testing

✅ Benefits

  • Reduces rework and cost overruns
  • Improves communication across teams
  • Enables traceability from need to test
  • Supports safety and certification

⚠️ Without RE

  • Ambiguous specifications
  • Missed safety constraints
  • Hardware/software mismatches
  • Untestable deliverables

Types of Requirements

TypeDefinitionEmbedded Example
Stakeholder RequirementsHigh-level needs expressed by users, customers, or operators"The system must alert the operator when temperature exceeds safe limits."
System RequirementsWhat the complete system (HW+SW) must do"The system shall activate the cooling fan within 500 ms of detecting T > 75 °C."
Software/Firmware RequirementsBehavior of the software/firmware component"The firmware shall poll the temperature sensor every 200 ms."
Hardware RequirementsPhysical and electrical constraints on hardware"The MCU shall operate at 3.3 V ± 5% supply voltage."

Functional vs. Non-Functional Requirements

Functional Requirements (FR) – What the system does.
Example: "The system shall display the current temperature on the LCD."
Non-Functional Requirements (NFR) – How well the system does it.
Example: "The system shall respond to a temperature change within 500 ms."

Stakeholders in Embedded RE

  • End Users / Operators – Use the system daily
  • System Architects – Define overall system structure
  • Hardware Engineers – Design PCB, select components
  • Firmware/Software Engineers – Implement control logic
  • QA/Test Engineers – Verify and validate requirements
  • Safety/Regulatory Engineers – Ensure compliance with applicable standards
  • Project Managers – Manage scope, schedule, cost
  • Customers/Clients – Define business needs

RE and the Embedded Development Life Cycle

Requirements Engineering feeds directly into system architecture, hardware design, firmware development, integration testing, and product maintenance. Changes at any stage may require revisiting requirements. RE is therefore an ongoing activity, not a one-time phase.

Learning Objectives

  1. Define requirements engineering and explain its role in embedded system development.
  2. Distinguish functional, non-functional, hardware, software, interface, timing, safety, and power requirements.
  3. Apply elicitation techniques to gather stakeholder needs.
  4. Write clear, unambiguous, testable requirements using standard identifiers.
  5. Construct an SRS document for an embedded system.
  6. Build and interpret a Requirement Traceability Matrix (RTM).
  7. Distinguish verification from validation and derive test cases from requirements.
  8. Manage requirement changes and perform impact analysis.
  9. Apply MoSCoW prioritization to embedded requirements.
  10. Prepare for GATE examinations and embedded systems engineering interviews.

Feasibility – Whether a requirement can be implemented within project constraints.

2. Requirements Engineering Process

The RE process is iterative, not strictly sequential. Teams often revisit earlier stages as new information emerges, stakeholders change, or technology constraints are discovered.

Definition: The process of discovering, collecting, and understanding stakeholder needs, goals, and constraints.

Activities: Interviews, workshops, observation, document review, prototyping, use-case analysis.

Tools: Interview guides, questionnaires, use-case templates, affinity diagrams.

Output: Stakeholder needs list, raw requirement statements, use-case descriptions.

Challenges: Stakeholders may not know what they want; conflicting needs; tacit knowledge; communication barriers.

Embedded Example: Interviewing a factory floor operator reveals that the cooling system must continue operating during a brief power interruption (UPS requirement not initially stated).

Definition: Examining raw requirements to resolve conflicts, remove ambiguity, check feasibility, and model system behavior.

Activities: Conflict resolution, feasibility assessment, modeling (state machines, DFDs), classification (FR/NFR/HW).

Tools: UML tools, state-machine editors, spreadsheets, FMEA worksheets.

Output: Analyzed, classified, and prioritized requirement list; system models.

Challenges: Incomplete information; hardware/software boundary decisions; conflicting stakeholder priorities.

Embedded Example: Analysis reveals that a 100 ms fan response time requires a sensor sampling interval of ≤ 50 ms, which affects MCU selection.

Definition: Formally documenting requirements in a structured, unambiguous, and verifiable form.

Activities: Writing SRS, assigning unique IDs, defining acceptance criteria, linking to stakeholder needs.

Tools: SRS templates (IEEE 830 / ISO/IEC/IEEE 29148 as guidance), DOORS, Jira, Confluence.

Output: Software Requirements Specification (SRS) document.

Challenges: Maintaining consistency; avoiding vague language ("fast", "reliable"); keeping the document up to date.

Embedded Example: FR-003: "The firmware shall activate the cooling fan within 500 ms (±50 ms) of the temperature sensor reading exceeding 75 °C, under normal operating conditions (5–40 °C ambient, nominal supply voltage)."

Definition: Confirming that the documented requirements correctly represent stakeholder needs ("Are we building the right system?").

Activities: Stakeholder reviews, walkthroughs, prototyping, scenario testing.

Tools: Review checklists, prototype demos, acceptance test plans.

Output: Validated SRS with stakeholder sign-off; list of issues to resolve.

Challenges: Stakeholders may not read technical documents; late discovery of missing requirements.

Embedded Example: A prototype demo reveals that the operator expects an audible alarm, not just an LED indicator — a requirement that was missed during elicitation.

Definition: Checking that requirements are correct, complete, consistent, and testable ("Are we building it right?").

Activities: Formal inspections, peer reviews, automated consistency checks, test-case derivation.

Tools: Review checklists, static analysis tools, traceability tools.

Output: Reviewed SRS; defect list; updated requirements.

Challenges: Time pressure; reviewer expertise; large document size.

Embedded Example: A review finds that FR-005 references a sensor accuracy of ±0.5 °C but HW-002 specifies a sensor with ±1 °C accuracy — an inconsistency requiring resolution.

Definition: Ranking requirements by importance, risk, and implementation effort to guide development planning.

Activities: MoSCoW analysis, risk assessment, stakeholder voting, cost-benefit analysis.

Tools: Priority matrices, MoSCoW tables, weighted scoring.

Output: Prioritized requirement list; release plan.

Challenges: All stakeholders want their requirements as "Must Have"; political pressure.

Embedded Example: Safety alarm (SAF-001) is Must Have; Bluetooth logging (COM-002) is Could Have for the first product release.

Definition: Ongoing process of tracking, controlling, and communicating requirement status throughout the project lifecycle.

Activities: Version control, change request processing, status tracking, stakeholder communication.

Tools: DOORS, Jira, Confluence, Git-based requirement tracking.

Output: Requirement status reports; change log; updated SRS versions.

Challenges: Scope creep; uncontrolled changes; keeping all teams synchronized.

Embedded Example: A change request to reduce fan response time from 500 ms to 200 ms is logged, analyzed for impact on firmware and hardware, approved, and communicated to all teams.

Definition: The ability to link each requirement to its origin (stakeholder need), design component, implementation, and test case.

Activities: Building RTM, linking requirements to design artifacts, test cases, and code modules.

Tools: RTM spreadsheets, DOORS, Polarion, Jira with traceability plugins.

Output: Requirement Traceability Matrix (RTM).

Challenges: Keeping RTM up to date; large systems with hundreds of requirements.

Embedded Example: FR-003 (fan activation) is traced to: Stakeholder Need SN-02 → Design Component: FanController module → Test Case TC-003.

Definition: Systematic process for evaluating, approving, and implementing changes to baselined requirements.

Activities: Change request submission, impact analysis, approval board review, implementation, re-testing.

Tools: Change control boards (CCB), issue trackers, configuration management tools.

Output: Approved change requests; updated SRS; updated test cases; change log.

Challenges: Cascading impacts across hardware, firmware, and tests; schedule and cost implications.

Embedded Example: Reducing fan response time from 500 ms to 200 ms requires: faster sensor sampling (firmware change), higher MCU clock (hardware change), updated test cases, and revised power budget.

3. Case Study: Smart Temperature Monitoring & Automatic Cooling System

Note: Threshold values (e.g., 75 °C, 85 °C) and timing values (e.g., 500 ms) used throughout this case study are illustrative design assumptions chosen for teaching purposes. Actual product specifications must be derived from validated engineering analysis and stakeholder requirements.

System Description

A smart temperature monitoring and automatic cooling system continuously measures the ambient or component temperature, activates a cooling fan when the temperature exceeds a defined threshold, displays the current temperature, and raises an alarm if the temperature reaches a critical level or if a sensor fault is detected.

4. Software Requirements Specification (SRS)

An SRS is the primary contract between stakeholders and the development team. It must be clear, complete, consistent, and verifiable. ISO/IEC/IEEE 29148:2018 provides guidance on requirements engineering processes and SRS structure — it is a reference standard, not a mandatory certification requirement unless specified by the project or customer.

Sample SRS: Smart Temperature Monitoring & Cooling System

1. Introduction

This document specifies the requirements for the Smart Temperature Monitoring and Automatic Cooling System (STMACS). It is intended for use by hardware engineers, firmware developers, QA engineers, and project managers.

2. Purpose & Scope

The purpose of STMACS is to monitor ambient or component temperature, automatically activate cooling when required, alert operators of critical conditions, and handle sensor faults safely. The system is intended for use in industrial enclosures and electronic equipment cabinets.

3. System Overview

STMACS consists of a temperature sensor, microcontroller, cooling fan, LCD display, alarm module, and optional UART communication interface. The system operates on a 5 V DC regulated supply.

13. Assumptions & Dependencies

  • The power supply provides stable 5 V ± 5% DC at all times during normal operation.
  • The temperature sensor is correctly installed and calibrated before deployment.
  • The ambient temperature of the installation environment is within 0–50 °C.
  • Threshold values (75 °C, 85 °C, 70 °C) are illustrative and must be confirmed with the customer for the actual product.

  • All Must Have functional requirements (FR-001 to FR-008) pass their corresponding test cases.

  • System initializes within 2 seconds in all test runs.

5. Functional & Non-Functional Requirements

Functional Requirements

Describe what the system does — its behaviors, functions, and responses to inputs.

  • System Inputs/Outputs: Sensor readings, fan control signals, display output
  • Control Logic: Threshold comparison, hysteresis, state transitions
  • Sensor Data Processing: ADC conversion, filtering, calibration offset
  • Fan Activation/Deactivation: Trigger conditions, PWM duty cycle
  • User Interaction: Alarm acknowledgment, configuration input
  • Error Handling: Sensor fault detection, watchdog recovery
  • Communication: UART data transmission format and rate

Non-Functional Requirements

Describe how well the system performs its functions — quality attributes and constraints.

  • Response Time: End-to-end latency from event to action
  • Reliability: MTBF, availability percentage
  • Power Consumption: Idle and active current limits
  • Memory Constraints: Flash and RAM usage limits
  • Environmental: Operating temperature, humidity range
  • Maintainability: Modular firmware, documented interfaces
  • Safety: Fail-safe behavior, fault detection coverage
  • Real-Time Behavior: Deterministic execution, deadline compliance

Why NFRs must be measurable: "The system shall be fast" is untestable. "The system shall activate the fan within 500 ms" is testable. Always attach a metric, unit, and condition to non-functional requirements.

Common Exam Questions

  • Q: Is "The system shall log temperature data" functional or non-functional? → Functional (it describes a behavior).
  • Q: Is "The system shall use less than 50 mA in idle mode" functional or non-functional? → Non-Functional (power constraint).
  • Q: Can a requirement be both? → Yes, a requirement may have both functional and non-functional aspects; they should be separated into distinct statements.

Resolving Conflicting Stakeholder Expectations

  1. Identify the conflict explicitly — document both positions.
  2. Understand the underlying need — often conflicts are about solutions, not goals.
  3. Prioritize by safety and business impact — safety requirements take precedence.
  4. Negotiate a compromise — e.g., configurable thresholds satisfy both strict and lenient users.
  5. Escalate to a decision authority if consensus cannot be reached.
  6. Document the decision and rationale in the SRS.
Example: The operator wants the fan to activate at 70 °C (conservative), but the hardware engineer says 80 °C is needed to avoid unnecessary wear. Resolution: Set activation at 75 °C with a configurable range of 65–80 °C, documented as a design decision.

Traceability Concepts

Forward Traceability: From stakeholder need → requirement → design → code → test. Ensures every need is implemented and tested.
Backward Traceability: From test → code → design → requirement → stakeholder need. Ensures every design element and test has a justified requirement.

How traceability helps:

  • Missing requirements: A design component with no linked requirement may be gold-plating or an undocumented need.
  • Untested functionality: A requirement with no test case ID is a coverage gap.
  • Change impact: Changing FR-003 (fan threshold) immediately shows which test cases (TC-003, TC-004) and design components (FanController) are affected.

10. Requirements Verification & Validation

Verification – "Are we building the system right?"
Checks that the system conforms to its specification. Methods: inspection, analysis, demonstration, testing.
Validation – "Are we building the right system?"
Checks that the system meets stakeholder needs. Methods: acceptance testing, user trials, prototyping.

Change Management Process

1. Change Request
Stakeholder submits a formal change request with justification.
2. Impact Analysis
Team assesses effect on requirements, design, firmware, hardware, tests, schedule, and cost.
3. Approval (CCB)
Change Control Board reviews and approves, rejects, or defers the change.

Affected test cases are re-executed; RTM is updated.

Example 3: Requirement Verification Coverage

Calculate the percentage of requirements that have been verified.

Formula:

Coverage (%) = (Number of requirements verified / Total applicable requirements) × 100

Given (illustrative):

  • Total applicable requirements in SRS: 20
  • Requirements with completed test cases and passing results: 12
  • Requirements deferred to next release: 2
  • Applicable requirements for current release: 20 − 2 = 18

Calculation:

Coverage = (12 / 18) × 100 = 66.7%
Limitation: Coverage percentage alone does not prove the system is correct or safe. A requirement may "pass" a poorly designed test case. Coverage must be combined with test quality assessment, fault injection testing, and safety analysis to provide meaningful assurance.

GATE-Style Numerical Question

Q: A temperature monitoring system has 25 functional requirements, 8 non-functional requirements, and 5 hardware requirements. During testing, 20 functional, 5 non-functional, and 4 hardware requirements have been verified. What is the overall requirement verification coverage?

Solution:

  • Total requirements = 25 + 8 + 5 = 38
  • Verified requirements = 20 + 5 + 4 = 29
  • Coverage = (29 / 38) × 100 = 76.3%

Answer: 76.3% (approximately 76%)

Scenario-Based Question

Q: A firmware engineer changes the sensor sampling interval from 200 ms to 80 ms to meet a new 200 ms fan response time requirement. List three other items in the project that must be reviewed and potentially updated.

Answer:

  1. Power consumption (NFR-003): Higher sampling rate increases MCU active time and current draw — the 50 mA idle limit may be violated.
  2. Test cases (TC-001, TC-003): TC-001 pass criterion (≥ 50 readings in 10 s) must be updated to ≥ 125 readings in 10 s; TC-003 pass criterion changes from ≤ 500 ms to ≤ 200 ms.
  3. MCU workload analysis: Increased sampling frequency may affect other firmware tasks — a CPU utilization analysis is required to ensure no task deadlines are missed.

14. Embedded Systems Interview Preparation

Expand each question to see a concise answer suitable for embedded software, firmware, automotive, IoT, and semiconductor company interviews.
Q1: What is requirements engineering and why is it important in embedded systems? ▶

Requirements engineering is the systematic process of eliciting, analyzing, specifying, validating, and managing the needs and constraints that a system must satisfy. In embedded systems, it is critical because: (1) hardware and software are tightly coupled — a missed requirement can require costly PCB redesign; (2) real-time constraints must be precisely defined to ensure correct behavior; (3) safety-critical systems require traceable, verifiable requirements for certification; (4) fixing a defect found in the field costs orders of magnitude more than fixing it during requirements.

Follow-up: "Can you give an example of a requirement that, if missed, could cause a field failure?" → Missing a sensor fault detection requirement could cause a cooling system to fail silently, leading to equipment overheating.

Q2: What is the difference between functional and non-functional requirements? ▶

Functional requirements describe what the system does — its behaviors, inputs, outputs, and functions. Example: "The system shall activate the fan when temperature exceeds 75 °C." Non-functional requirements describe how well the system performs — quality attributes and constraints. Example: "The fan shall activate within 500 ms." Both are equally important; missing NFRs often cause performance, safety, or reliability failures in the field.

Q3: What makes an embedded requirement testable? ▶

A testable requirement must have: (1) a clear trigger or precondition; (2) a measurable expected outcome with units; (3) defined operating conditions; (4) a feasible verification method. Example: "The firmware shall activate the fan within 500 ms (±50 ms) of the temperature exceeding 75 °C, measured from threshold crossing to fan GPIO signal assertion, under nominal supply voltage and 25 °C ambient." This can be verified with an oscilloscope and a temperature source.

Q4: What is an SRS and what should it contain? ▶

An SRS (Software Requirements Specification) is the primary document that formally describes all requirements for a system. It should contain: system overview and scope, stakeholder identification, functional requirements (with unique IDs), non-functional requirements, hardware requirements, interface requirements, timing and performance constraints, safety and security requirements, environmental constraints, assumptions and dependencies, and acceptance criteria. Each requirement should be uniquely identified, unambiguous, and verifiable.

Q5: What is a Requirement Traceability Matrix and how do you use it? ▶

An RTM is a table that links each requirement to its source (stakeholder need), design component, implementation artifact, test case, and verification status. Forward traceability (need → test) ensures every requirement is implemented and tested. Backward traceability (test → need) ensures every design element has a justified requirement. I use the RTM to identify coverage gaps (requirements without test cases), gold-plating (design elements without requirements), and to perform impact analysis when a requirement changes.

Q6: How are real-time constraints specified in embedded requirements? ▶

Real-time constraints are specified as timing requirements with: (1) a trigger event (e.g., temperature threshold crossing); (2) a deadline (e.g., ≤ 500 ms); (3) operating conditions (e.g., nominal supply, specified temperature range); (4) a verification method (e.g., oscilloscope measurement). The timing budget should be broken down into components (sensor conversion, firmware processing, actuator switching) to verify feasibility during design. Worst-case execution time (WCET) analysis is used for hard real-time systems.

Q7: What is the difference between verification and validation? ▶

Verification asks "Are we building the system right?" — it checks that the system conforms to its specification. Methods include inspection, analysis, demonstration, and testing. Validation asks "Are we building the right system?" — it checks that the system meets actual stakeholder needs. Methods include acceptance testing, user trials, and prototyping. A system can pass verification (meets the SRS) but fail validation (the SRS didn't capture the real need). Both are necessary.

Q8: How do you manage changing requirements in an embedded project? ▶

I follow a formal change management process: (1) the change is submitted as a Change Request with justification; (2) impact analysis is performed — which requirements, design components, firmware modules, test cases, and schedule items are affected; (3) the Change Control Board reviews and approves/rejects/defers; (4) approved changes are implemented, the SRS is updated with a new version number, and affected test cases are re-executed; (5) the RTM is updated. Uncontrolled changes are the leading cause of scope creep and integration failures.

Q9: How do you handle conflicting stakeholder requirements? ▶

I first document both positions explicitly. Then I try to understand the underlying need behind each position — often the conflict is about proposed solutions, not actual goals. I prioritize by safety and business impact (safety requirements always take precedence). I facilitate a negotiation session to find a compromise (e.g., configurable thresholds). If consensus cannot be reached, I escalate to a decision authority. The final decision and its rationale are documented in the SRS.

Q10: How do incomplete requirements cause field failures? ▶

Incomplete requirements leave gaps that engineers fill with assumptions — which may be wrong. Examples: (1) No sensor fault requirement → firmware has no fault detection → system silently fails when sensor disconnects → equipment overheats; (2) No power-on initialization requirement → system starts in an undefined state → intermittent failures in the field; (3) No environmental requirement → system tested at 25 °C but deployed at 50 °C → timing violations and component failures. Thorough elicitation, formal reviews, and traceability audits reduce this risk.

Q11: How do safety-related requirements differ from regular requirements? ▶

Safety requirements define fail-safe behavior, fault detection, and system response to hazardous conditions. They are typically: (1) assigned the highest priority (Must Have); (2) subject to more rigorous verification (e.g., fault injection testing); (3) documented with explicit rationale and hazard references; (4) reviewed by a safety engineer; (5) potentially subject to applicable safety standards (e.g., IEC 61508 for functional safety — only when required by the project). Example: SAF-001 requires the fan to activate at maximum speed if the sensor fails, preventing overheating even when the primary control path is unavailable.

17. Quick Revision Sheet

Requirements Engineering Process Summary

  1. Elicitation → Gather stakeholder needs (interviews, observation, workshops)
  2. Analysis → Resolve conflicts, model behavior, check feasibility
  3. Specification → Write SRS with unique IDs, clear language, acceptance criteria
  4. Validation → Confirm requirements match stakeholder needs
  5. Verification → Confirm requirements are correct, complete, consistent, testable
  6. Prioritization → MoSCoW ranking
  7. Management → Track status, control changes, maintain versions
  8. Traceability → RTM linking need → requirement → design → test

SRS Checklist

  • Every requirement has a unique ID
  • Every requirement is testable (has a measurable criterion)
  • No requirement contains "and" joining two separate requirements
  • No vague terms: "fast", "reliable", "usually", "appropriate"
  • All requirements are traceable to a stakeholder need
  • Safety requirements are identified and have fail-safe behavior defined
  • Timing requirements include trigger, deadline, and operating conditions
  • Assumptions and dependencies are documented
  • Acceptance criteria are defined for all Must Have requirements

Common Examination Mistakes

  • Confusing verification ("built it right") with validation ("built the right thing")
  • Classifying power consumption as a functional requirement (it is non-functional)
  • Writing non-atomic requirements (multiple requirements in one statement)
  • Forgetting that NFRs must be measurable to be verifiable
  • Confusing MoSCoW "Must Have" with "high priority" — Must Have means the system fails without it
  • Assuming 100% coverage means the system is correct — test quality matters
  • Forgetting that RE is iterative, not a one-time phase
